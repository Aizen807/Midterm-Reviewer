Ch. 1 — The Self from Various Perspectives

Philosophy, sociology, and psychology each answer "who am I?" differently. This chapter walks through six philosophers, the sociology of the social self, and five psychological theories of self.

Lesson 1 — Philosophical Perspectives

+

Socrates

"Know thyself" — an unexamined life is not worth living. Self-knowledge comes from your own firsthand experience: observing, questioning, reasoning. He believed a person is body + soul, and the soul (immortal, perfect) is our true self. Knowledge is the ultimate virtue; the only evil is ignorance. He never wrote anything down — we know him through his student, Plato.

Plato

The soul has three parts: reason (truth/good), spirit (honor, emotion), and appetite (food, drink, sex) — happiness comes from balancing them. He also split reality into the visible world (senses, imperfect appearances) and the non-visible world of ideal Forms, graspable only by the mind.

René Descartes

"Father of Modern Philosophy." Famous line: Cogito ergo sum — "I think, therefore I am." Even doubting everything proves an "I" exists to do the doubting. He also proposed Cartesian Dualism: mind and body are separate, distinct substances.

David Hume

An empiricist — all knowledge comes from sense experience. The self is not a fixed thing but a "bundle of perceptions" in constant flux. Impressions (vivid, direct experience) come first; ideas are just faded copies of impressions.

Immanuel Kant

People have intrinsic worth because they're rational agents who can direct their own actions. Moral rule: treat people always as an end in themselves, never merely as a means.

John Locke

Personal identity = psychological continuity — it's founded on consciousness/memory, not on the soul or the body. This is his Theory of Personal Identity, addressing survival of the self over time (and after death).

Lesson 2 — The Self and Society

+

Charles Horton Cooley — The Looking-Glass Self

We learn who we are by imagining how others see us. Three phases:

  • 1. We imagine how we appear to others.
  • 2. We imagine their judgment of that appearance.
  • 3. We develop a feeling about ourselves based on that imagined judgment.

Because this is based on our imagination of others' views (which can be wrong), the self can form around misperceptions — but self-identities can also change.

George Herbert Mead — The Social Self

The self emerges through social interaction and is made of two parts:

  • "I" — the self as subject; the spontaneous, acting self.
  • "Me" — the self as object; the image of yourself built from how society sees you.

Mead's three stages of self-development:

  • Preparation stage — children imitate people around them (no real understanding yet).
  • Play stage — children pretend to be other people; role-taking begins.
  • Game stage — children grasp multiple roles/relationships at once, understanding their own position and others' simultaneously.

The Self and Culture

Culture = practices, beliefs, and behaviors passed down generationally. It shapes how we think, feel, and behave, and the development of the self varies from society to society.

Lesson 3 — The Psychological View of the Self

+

Sigmund Freud — Structure of Personality

  • Id — pleasure principle; selfish, illogical, amoral (not immoral); seeks immediate gratification.
  • Ego — reality principle; the only part in contact with the real world; mediator.
  • Superego — represents society's/parents' values and standards; the conscience.

Defense Mechanisms (Ego's coping tools)

  • Rationalization — inventing a logical-sounding excuse ("I cut class because others do too").
  • Denial — refusing to accept a fact or feeling exists.
  • Repression — unconsciously blocking out painful thoughts/memories.
  • Projection — attributing your own unwanted feelings to someone else.
  • Reaction formation — expressing the opposite of what you truly feel.
  • Sublimation — channeling an impulse into something socially acceptable.
  • Displacement — redirecting emotion from its real target to a safer one (e.g., yelling at the dog after a bad day).

William James — "I" and "Me"

Four constituents of the self: material, social, spiritual self (together = the "Me" / Empirical Self), and the pure ego (the "I" — the knower, the sense of personal identity).

Carl Rogers — Real Self vs. Ideal Self

Real self = who you truly are. Ideal self = who you feel you should be (shaped by outside expectations). A big gap between the two causes stress and anxiety.

Donald Winnicott — Real (True) Self vs. False Self

The false self develops when an infant's emotional needs are unmet or met unreliably, creating basic mistrust. Adults wear "social masks" (false self) to protect the true self from a demanding world — useful short-term, but overuse blocks genuine connection.

Ch. 2 — Unpacking the Self

Six aspects of the self are examined one by one: physical, sexual, material, spiritual, political, and digital.

Lesson 1 — The Physical Self

+

Our body image is shaped by media (unrealistic beauty standards), peers (childhood nicknames/labels), and adults around us (their own body insecurities rub off on us).

Self-Concept — 3 Ingredients

  • Self-Ideal — the picture of the ideal person you'd most like to be.
  • Self-Image — how you picture yourself performing; your "inner mirror."
  • Self-Esteem — the root of self-concept; how you feel about yourself.

Maintaining a Healthy Mental Attitude

Daily affirmations, physical activity, hydration, a balanced diet, and supportive relationships can contribute to well-being.

Lesson 2 — The Sexual Self

+

Primary vs. Secondary Sex Characteristics

Primary = the reproductive organs themselves (ovaries/uterus/vagina; testes/penis/scrotum). Secondary = physical signs of maturity not directly tied to reproduction (breast development, voice change, body hair, muscle growth) — driven by testosterone (males) and estrogen (females).

Key milestones: spermarche (first ejaculation, ~age 13) and menarche (first menstrual period).

Erogenous Zones

Body parts especially sensitive to touch that produce sexual arousal (mouth, breasts, sex organs, anus — varies per person; also neck, thighs, abdomen, feet).

Biology of Sexual Behavior — Helen Fisher's 3 Systems

  • Lust (sex drive) — driven by estrogens/androgens; craving for gratification.
  • Attraction ("passionate love") — high dopamine & norepinephrine, low serotonin; focused energy on one partner.
  • Attachment ("companionate love") — oxytocin & vasopressin; calm, secure bonding.

Sexual Response Cycle (Masters & Johnson)

Four phases: Excitement → Plateau → Orgasm → Resolution.

Lesson 3 — The Material Self

+

From William James: the material self = everything a person can call "mine." Four components, from innermost to outermost:

  • Body — the innermost part of the material self.
  • Clothes — shape identity and communicate who we are.
  • Immediate family — their success/failure feels like our own.
  • Home — decorated as an extension of ourselves.

Key idea: the material self isn't the object itself — it's our psychological ownership of it. Test: how much emotional investment and behavior-shaping the object has on you.

Religion (Durkheim) = a unified system of beliefs/practices about sacred things, uniting a moral community. Ritual = customary ceremonies (birth, baptism, marriage, death) that bind religious communities together.

Logotherapy (Viktor Frankl)

"Therapy through meaning," developed after Frankl survived Nazi concentration camps. Core belief: humans are driven by the search for life's meaning, even amid suffering. Meaning can be found by (1) creating a work/accomplishing a task, (2) experiencing something or someone, or (3) choosing one's attitude toward unavoidable suffering.

Lesson 5 — The Political Self (Filipino Identity)

+

Strengths of the Filipino Character

  • Pakikipagkapwa-tao — sense of justice, empathy, hospitality, bayanihan, utang na loob.
  • Family orientation — honor to parents/elders; family as main source of identity and support.
  • Joy and humor — love of celebration, laughing even in hard times.
  • Flexibility, adaptability & creativity — resourcefulness, adapting anywhere.
  • Hard work and industry — willingness to take risky jobs, work multiple jobs.
  • Faith and religiosity — deep faith in God's will.
  • Ability to survive — perseverance despite hardship.

Weaknesses of the Filipino Character

  • Extreme personalism — takes everything personally; discomfort with impersonal rules/bureaucracy.
  • Extreme family-centeredness — nepotism, political dynasties, protecting erring relatives.
  • Lack of discipline — poor time management, procrastination ("Filipino time").
  • Passivity and lack of initiative — waiting to be told, over-reliance on others, submissive to authority.
  • Colonial mentality — preference for foreign things; feeling of national inferiority.
  • Kanya-kanya syndrome — selfish, self-serving, envious of peers' success.
  • Lack of self-analysis/reflection — superficial handling of serious problems.

Lesson 6 — The Digital Self

+

Digital Self (Russell Belk) — the persona we project online through our digital possessions (photos, videos, posts). Donald Winnicott's true self/false self applies here too: the "false self" can dominate online when we overproject an image to impress others.

Goffman's Self-Presentation & Impression Management

People consciously "perform" identities for others. Impression management = consciously/subconsciously shaping how others perceive you. Jones & Pittman's 5 strategies:

  • Ingratiation — being likable to gain affection.
  • Self-promotion — highlighting strengths/talents to look competent.
  • Exemplification — showing exemplary behavior to look dedicated.
  • Intimidation — using threats to appear powerful.
  • Supplication — acting weak/helpless to get help.

Sex vs. Gender vs. Sexuality

  • Sex — biological/physical makeup (organs, genes, hormones).
  • Gender (WHO) — socially constructed roles/norms; varies by society and can change.
  • Sexuality — a broad aspect of being human that includes attraction, orientation, desires, feelings, experiences, values, behavior, and relationships.

Setting Boundaries Online

Be kind (anti-bullying); think before you post (nothing is truly erasable); carefully choose who you friend/accept online.

Ch. 3 — Managing and Caring for the Self

How we learn, set goals, and manage stress and health — the practical toolkit for becoming a better, healthier student.

Lesson 1 — Learning to Be a Better Student

+

Learning = a relatively permanent change in behavior from experience (not from maturation or fatigue). Three main types: associative, nonassociative, and observational.

Associative Learning

  • Classical conditioning (Pavlov) — pairing a neutral stimulus with one that naturally triggers a response, until the neutral one triggers it alone. (Bell + meat → dog salivates to bell alone.) Terms: UCS/UCR (unlearned) vs. CS/CR (learned).
  • Operant conditioning — learning through consequences of voluntary behavior.
    • Positive reinforcement — adding something pleasant to increase behavior.
    • Negative reinforcement — removing something unpleasant to increase behavior.
    • Positive punishment — adding something unpleasant to decrease behavior.
    • Negative punishment — removing something pleasant to decrease behavior.

Nonassociative Learning

  • Habituation — decreased response to a repeated, harmless stimulus (getting used to a new bed).
  • Sensitization — increased response to stimuli after one strong/traumatic stimulus (jumpy after a typhoon).

Observational Learning (Bandura)

Learning by watching a model — four processes: attention → retention → motor reproduction → reinforcement.

Metacognition

"Thinking about thinking" — awareness of what you know and don't know. Two key strategies: self-instruction (self-talk through a task) and self-monitoring (checking your own performance, e.g., with a checklist).

Lesson 1B — Setting Goals for Success

+

Self-Efficacy (Albert Bandura)

Belief in your ability to influence events in your life. Anyone can build it, via 4 sources:

  • Mastery experiences — succeeding firsthand builds it most.
  • Social modeling — watching similar others succeed.
  • Social persuasion — encouragement from mentors/parents/coaches.
  • States of physiology — your emotional/physical state colors your confidence.

Mindset Theory (Carol Dweck)

Fixed mindset — belief that intelligence/ability is innate and unchangeable. Growth mindset — belief that ability can be developed through effort; leads to higher achievement.

Goal-Setting Theory (Edwin Locke & Gary Latham)

Specific + challenging goals with feedback = better performance. Five keys: Clarity, Challenge, Commitment, Feedback, Task complexity (break big goals into smaller steps).

S.M.A.R.T. Goals: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound.

Lesson 1C — Taking Charge of One's Health

+

Stress Basics

Eustress = positive, alerting stress (e.g., before a presentation). Distress = harmful, prolonged stress that impairs functioning.

General Adaptation Syndrome (Hans Selye)

  1. Alarm stage — initial shock; immune resistance drops.
  2. Resistance stage — body fights back efficiently.
  3. Exhaustion stage — prolonged stress can increase vulnerability to illness and, in severe cases, contribute to serious harm.

The HPA axis (hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis) triggers cortisol release ("the stress hormone") in response to threats.

Sources of Stress

  • Daily hassles (household, health, time-pressure, environmental, financial, work, security) vs. uplifts (small pleasant events).
  • Life changes — even positive changes (graduating, new job) require adjustment and can be stressful.
  • Conflict types:
    • Approach-approach — choosing between 2 desirable goals (least stressful).
    • Avoidance-avoidance — stuck between 2 undesirable options.
    • Approach-avoidance — one goal has both good and bad aspects.
    • Multiple approach-avoidance — several options, each with pros and cons (most complex).

Personality & Stress

  • Type A — driven, impatient, competitive; earns more but at higher health risk.
  • Type B — relaxed, less impatient, paced.
  • Type D ("distressed") — negative emotions + social inhibition; higher cardiovascular risk.

Psychological Moderators of Stress

Self-efficacy, psychological hardiness (commitment, control, challenge — Suzanne Kobasa), sense of humor, predictability & control, and social support (emotional, instrumental, informational, appraisal, socializing).

Stress Management Strategies

  • Physical — exercise, balanced diet, avoid caffeine, sleep, hydration.
  • Behavioral — diversionary hobbies (art, gardening, writing).
  • Cognitive restructuring — challenge catastrophic thinking by asking: What's the worst that could happen? How likely is it really? Have I survived worse before?

Socrates “Know thyself” is Socrates’ famous line. This means that man must stand and live according to his nature. He has to examine his life to understand his inner self. Socrates asserts that an unexamined life is not worth living. Hence, whatever experiences, good or bad, we go through, we should think about the reason why they happen, examining our actions and understanding the pattern of our behaviors to guide us what needs to be modified or changed. Socrates believes that the quality and persistence of the attention we pay to living an examined life is at the heart of living well. He further asserts that our self – discovery and self – knowledge must be done through our won first hand experiences of life. Tapping our capacity to observe, question, reason, and create may lead to discovering errors that would inspire and help us create new better ideas. Socrates also believes that knowledge is the ultimate virtue, best used to help people improve their lives. According to him, “The only good is knowledge and the only evil is ignorance”. Unlike the philosophers, who came before him and during his time who sought to learn how the world works, Socrates pursued a different course of interest: he occupied himself with ethical matters to learn how people should behave. He urges people to strive after self – knowledge and find time to seriously consider how they ought to live. He believes that man is composed of a body and a soul. The soul is immortal and perfect, while the body is impermanent and decays after a person’s death. He further claimed that the true nature of our true self is our soul. The state of one’s soul determines the quality of one’s life. It is important to know what is good and what is evil. Socrates’ life is a demonstration of the two important principles that guided his thought and his actions: (1) his faithful adherence to the principle of never doing wrong or participating in any wrongdoing, even indirectly, and (2) his strong belief that a person who knows what is good and right could not act against it. Socrates’ never put into text his ideas because he was worried that if people had the answers before them, this would encourage them to stop thinking. He wanted them to think and arrive at their own understanding of the things they wanted to know and choose the best from all the generalizations that ensue from their discussions. The best source of our knowledge of Socrates is Plato, Socrates’ student who wrote the discourses of Socrates. Plato Plato was Socrates’ most famous student. Most of the things we know about Socrates come from the writings of Plato, who supported most of the ideas of Socrates. Plato agrees with Socrates that a person has a soul. According to Plato, the soul is composed of three parts: reason, spirit, and appetite. At an early age, we didn’t have so much understanding of who we were; however, as we grow older, we broaden our knowledge of ourselves. How did this happen? George Herbert Mead, a sociologist, is known for his theory of the social self. He postulates that our concept of who we are emerges as we interact with others. According to him, the self is a distinct identity that set us apart from others. It is not a static phenomenon, but continues to develop and change. This means that the self is constructed and reconstructed in the process of social experience and activity. To understand how an individual develops and modifies the sense of self as a result of social interaction, the work of Horton Cooley and George Herbert Mead pioneered the interactionist approach. Cooley believes that we learn who we are by interacting with others. This means that our view of ourselves comes not only from direct contemplation of our personal qualities but also from our impression of how others perceive us. As a product of our social interaction, the self is termed by Cooley as the looking – glass self. There are three phases of developing self – identity or self – concept. First is the imagination of how we present ourselves to others, our parents, friends, and strangers. Second is the imagination of how others evaluate us (intelligent, charismatic, and cheerful). Last is the development of some sort of feeling about ourselves as a result of these impressions. What is crucial in Cooley’s glass self is when we develop an imagination of how others view us. Repression is the unconscious blocking of unacceptable thought, feelings, and impulses. Projection means attributing one’s own undesired thoughts, feeling, or impulses to another person who does not have those thoughts, feelings, or impulses. Sublimation is the channeling of unacceptable impulses, thought, and emotions into more acceptable ones. Displacement is the channeling of energy away from one person or object to an alternative. Lesson 3 — The Muscular System: Types & Functions

+

The muscular system is specialized tissue that enables the body and its parts to move.

Trivia

  • There are about 640 muscles in the human body, making up about 40% of body mass.
  • Longest muscle: the Sartorius (runs from the outside of the hip, down and across to the inside of the knee).
  • Smallest muscle: the Stapedius (deep in the ear, only 5mm long, involved in hearing).
  • Biggest muscle: the Gluteus Maximus (in the buttock, pulls the leg backward powerfully for walking and running).

Functions of the Muscles

Movement; maintenance of posture and muscle tone; heat production; protects the bones and internal organs.

Muscle Classification

  • Functionally: Voluntary (can be moved at will) vs. Involuntary (can't be moved intentionally).
  • Structurally: Striated (has stripes across the fiber) vs. Smooth (no striations).

The 3 Types of Muscles

TypeCharacteristics
Smooth MuscleThin, spindle-shaped fibers; no striations; single nuclei; involuntary; contracts slowly, fatigues very slowly. Found in the circulatory system (blood vessel lining), digestive system (esophagus, stomach, intestine — controls digestion), respiratory system (controls breathing), and urinary system (bladder — controls urination).
Cardiac MuscleBranched cells fused with one another; has striations; central nuclei; involuntary. Found ONLY in the heart — contractions pump blood and account for the heartbeat. Healthy cardiac muscle never fatigues.
Skeletal MuscleLong, cylindrical fibers; many nuclei; striations with alternating dark and light bands; voluntary. Attached to the skeleton by tendons; causes movement of bones at the joints. Unlike cardiac muscle, it does fatigue.

Functions of Skeletal Muscle

  • Movement — muscles move bones by pulling, not pushing.
  • Synergists — all muscles responsible for a movement; the one most responsible is the Prime Mover (agonist).
  • Antagonists — muscle pairs that cause opposite actions (e.g., biceps flex the arm, triceps extend it); when one contracts, the other relaxes.
  • Levators — muscles that raise a body part.
  • Maintenance of posture/muscle tone — tonic contractions hold body position without producing movement.
  • Heat production — muscle contraction produces most of the heat needed to maintain body temperature.

Lesson 4 — Skeletal Muscle Structure & Actions

+

Structure

Composed of striated muscle cells (fibers) and connective tissue. Most muscles attach to two bones with a moveable joint between them:

  • Origin — the attachment to the bone that does NOT move.
  • Insertion — the attachment to the bone that moves.
  • Belly — the fleshy part of the muscle between the tendons of origin and insertion.
  • Tendons — dense fibrous connective tissue that anchors muscle firmly to bone.
  • Ligaments — connect bone to bone at a joint.
  • Bursae — small fluid-filled sacs between some tendons and the bones beneath them, lined with synovial membrane that secretes synovial fluid.

Microscopic Anatomy & Contraction

The membrane around a muscle cell is the sarcolemma. Muscle cells contain myofilaments — myosin (thick) and actin (thin) — arranged in units called sarcomeres. At the neuromuscular junction, the space between a motor nerve's axon and the muscle fiber is the synapse, where the neurotransmitter acetylcholine is released.

Contraction sequence: an electrical impulse travels down a motor neuron → acetylcholine is released into the synapse → it binds receptors on the muscle cell → an electrical impulse spreads over the cell → sarcomeres shorten → the muscle contracts.

Skeletal muscles always work in pairs, with two possible movements: Contraction (shorten) and Extension (lengthen).

AdductorMoves limb toward the midline of the body

Naming Skeletal Muscles

Muscles are named by location (e.g., Tibialis anterior — front of tibia), shape (deltoid = triangle, trapezius = trapezoid, rhomboideus = rhomboid), relative size (maximus = largest, minimis = smallest, longus = longest, brevis = short), direction of fibers (rectus = straight/parallel to long axis), number of origins (biceps = 2, triceps = 3, quadriceps = 4), location of attachments (e.g., sternocleidomastoid — origin at sternum/clavicle, insertion at the mastoid process), or action (e.g., flexor carpi radialis flexes the wrist).

Fun fact: there are about 60 muscles in the face — it takes 20 muscles to smile, and over 40 to frown.

Lesson 1 — What Is a Computer?

+

A computer is a machine that can be instructed to perform an operation. It is also used to store and process data.

The Data Processing Model

Data → Processing System → Information

Functions of an Information Processing System

  • Input — it accepts and gathers data.
  • Processing — it processes data to become information.
  • Storage — it stores data and information.
  • Output — it presents information.

Computer Lab Rules

No food or drink; keep your workstation clean; respect others and work quietly; don't change settings; keep your files organized; surf safely; save often; print only when necessary; log off when done.

Lesson 2 — The Three Major Elements of a Computer

+

Every computer system is made up of three major elements: Hardware, Software, and Peopleware.

Hardware

The tangible part of a computer system, or the exterior devices. A computer's hardware consists of electronic devices — the parts you can see and touch.

Software

The non-tangible part that tells the computer what and how to do its job. There are two main types:

  • System Software — controls a computer's internal functioning, chiefly through an operating system, and also controls peripherals such as monitors, printers, and storage devices.
  • Application Software — directs the computer to execute commands given by the user; includes any program that processes data for a user (e.g., Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Gmail, Paint).

Peopleware

Refers to people who use and operate the computer system, write computer programs, and analyze and design the information system.
